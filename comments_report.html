We appreciate the opportunity to provide input regarding the evaluation of bulk drug substances for inclusion on the Section 503A Bulks List.

As a clinical organization focused on improving metabolic health outcomes, we work closely with patients who benefit from individualized, provider-directed treatment plans. Compounded medications play an essential role in allowing for dosing flexibility, alternative formulations, and improved patient adherence—particularly in cases where commercially available products do not meet patient-specific needs.

We respectfully emphasize the importance of maintaining access to clinically relevant bulk drug substances that have demonstrated safety, efficacy, and therapeutic value when used under appropriate medical supervision. Limiting access to these substances may reduce providers’ ability to tailor treatments and could negatively impact patient outcomes, especially in populations requiring personalized care.

We encourage the Committee to continue evaluating nominated substances through a balanced, evidence-based framework that considers:

Clinical necessity and real-world patient outcomes
Safety profiles when compounded by compliant 503A pharmacies
The role of compounded therapies in addressing gaps in commercially available medications

We are committed to patient safety, regulatory compliance, and evidence-based practice. We support policies that uphold these standards while preserving appropriate access to compounded therapies that meaningfully improve patient care.

Thank you for your consideration.

My name is Jay, MSN, RN, PHN. I am a licensed registered nurse in the State of California with over 15 years of clinical experience, including credentials as an LVN, ADN, and MSN. I currently serve as Director of Nursing for a Home Health agency where I oversee patient care protocols, clinical compliance, and evidence-based practice implementation for a medically complex patient population.

I write in strong support of the inclusion of the following seven bulk drug substances on the Section 503A Bulk Drug Substances List: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon. I respectfully urge the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) to recommend their addition to Category 1, thereby restoring lawful access to these compounds through licensed compounding pharmacies operating under valid prescriptions from qualified practitioners.

The FDA's 2023 decision to classify these peptides as Category 2 substances effectively removed them from the compounding toolkit without a formal rulemaking process that weighed clinical benefit against risk. Practitioners and patients who had established safe, efficacious protocols were abruptly cut off from access. The July 23–24, 2026 PCAC review is an opportunity to correct that outcome through a rigorous, transparent, and clinically informed process.

Critically, Category 1 placement does not circumvent patient safety, it routes access through a framework of physician oversight, individualized prescriptions, and licensed pharmacy preparation. This is precisely the model that Section 503A was designed to support: personalized medicine, prepared safely, under the supervision of a qualified practitioner.

My support for the inclusion of these peptides is not a call for unregulated access. To the contrary, placing these substances on the Category 1 list restores order and accountability to what is currently an uncontrolled landscape. When legitimate compounding access is eliminated, patients do not simply forgo treatment. They seek alternatives through unregulated channels. Restoring lawful, prescription-based access through licensed 503A pharmacies is the most effective patient safety measure available to the FDA.

As a nurse and healthcare administrator, I am acutely aware of the consequences of regulatory gaps. The patients I serve deserve access to emerging therapies through safe, supervised, and compliant pathways.

I respectfully urge the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee to recommend the formal inclusion of BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon on the Section 503A Bulk Drug Substances List. The clinical evidence base, patient need, and existing prescriber demand collectively support this determination. The FDA should conclude its rulemaking promptly following the PCAC's recommendation and restore these compounds to compliant, practitioner-supervised use.

Thank you for the opportunity to submit this comment for the record.

TB-500 (free base / acetate) — Wound Healing
Chronic, non-healing wounds (e.g., diabetic ulcers, post-surgical complications) remain a significant challenge. For patients who fail standard wound care, clinicians often have limited alternatives. MOTs-C (free base / acetate) — Obesity and Osteoporosis
In obesity management, some patients cannot tolerate or have contraindications to approved medications. Similarly, osteoporosis treatments may be limited by side effects or access issues. Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, Epitalon — Neurologic and Sleep Disorders
There remains a need for alternatives in conditions such as opioid withdrawal, refractory insomnia, migraine, and trigeminal neuralgia, particularly where existing treatments are ineffective or poorly tolerated. Conclusion
Across these substances, the most relevant use cases involve refractory patients requiring individualized therapy after standard treatments have failed or are not tolerated. Compounded formulations may provide flexibility in dosing and administration that is not otherwise available.

I encourage the Committee to consider inclusion of these substances for limited, clinically appropriate indications, recognizing the role of physician judgment, informed consent, and careful monitoring in their use.

I am writing in support of preserving a lawful, medically supervised compounding pathway for the peptide-related bulk drug substances currently under review, including BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-C, Emideltide/DSIP, Semax, and Epitalon.

I understand that these substances raise legitimate questions about safety, quality, purity, appropriate use, and the limits of available clinical evidence. I am not asking FDA to ignore those concerns. I am asking FDA to address them through regulation, informed consent, and pharmacy quality standards rather than through restrictions that push patients into unregulated markets.

My position is grounded in my own experience. I have used peptides, and I have also had to navigate the gray market. I did not enter the gray market because I wanted to avoid medical supervision. I entered it because medically supervised access had become difficult, expensive, or unavailable. That distinction matters.

When patients lose access to compounded peptides through licensed pharmacies, many do not simply stop seeking them. They search online. They find vendors selling products labeled “for research use only” or “not for human consumption.” They compare dosing strategies in forums and private groups. They rely on anecdotal reports, incomplete information, and certificates of analysis of uncertain reliability. This is not a safer environment. It is a less visible one.

Restricting access does not eliminate demand. It often displaces demand into less regulated spaces.

A lawful compounding pathway gives patients, prescribers, and pharmacies a safer alternative, allowing individuals to seek compounds through a licensed clinician and compounding pharmacy rather than anonymous online suppliers. It supports sourcing, clearer labeling, informed consent, clinical monitoring, and adverse-event reporting.

For many, peptides represent an avenue of choice. People seek them for recovery from injury, support for inflammation, tissue repair, sleep, metabolic health, cognitive function, aging-related concerns, and overall quality of life. Clearly, not every claimed use is equally supported by evidence. Patient experience is not randomized clinical data. But patients often make medical decisions under uncertainty in consultation with clinicians after reviewing risks, benefits, alternatives, and unknowns. That same model should apply here.

Adults should be able to make informed choices about their own bodies when those choices are made with a prescriber and licensed pharmacy. A specific prescription, informed consent, and medical oversight should be sufficient for adults to access these compounds through regulated channels. Safeguards should be built into the pathway rather than used to eliminate the pathway.

Commercial pharmaceuticals may be unavailable, unaffordable, not covered by insurance, or not appropriate for a person’s specific needs. When access is limited only to branded products, a pharmaceutical paywall is created. That paywall determines who gets options and who does not.

Compounded therapies help dismantle that paywall. They provide more affordable, individualized options for people who otherwise may have no realistic avenue of access. Access and safety should not be treated as opposites. A well-regulated compounding pathway can advance both.

FDA should distinguish between irresponsible marketing and responsible patient-specific compounding. Misleading claims should be addressed. Products of unknown purity and sterility should not be promoted for human use. But licensed compounding pharmacies operating under appropriate standards are different. A patient-specific prescription written by a qualified clinician is different. A patient who has been informed of risks and uncertainties is different. The current environment creates a perverse incentive. Patients who want to be careful may have fewer lawful options than patients willing to take risks online. The system should reward responsible behavior by making the safer path easier to access than the unsafe path.

I respectfully urge FDA and the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee to support inclusion of the peptide-related bulk drug substances under review on the 503A Bulks List, subject to reasonable safeguards. Those safeguards should include prescription access, licensed compounding pharmacy preparation, appropriate sourcing and testing, clear labeling, informed consent, documentation of medical rationale, adverse-event monitoring, and restrictions on misleading marketing claims.

I am not asking FDA to pretend these compounds are risk-free. I am asking FDA to recognize that risk is better managed through lawful, supervised access than through prohibition or excessive restriction. Patients shouldn't be forced to choose between unaffordable pharmaceutical options and unregulated gray-market products. There should be a safer middle path. Licensed compounding pharmacies can provide that path.

Christopher Bailey
El Paso, Texas

SUBMISSION TO: FDA Public Docket No. FDA-2025-N-6895
RE: July 23-24, 2026 PCAC Meeting -- Bulk Drug Substances for the 503A Bulks List
DATE: May 28, 2026

To the Members of the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee:

I am a licensed 503A compounding pharmacy director with advanced credentials in Medicinal Biochemistry and Pharmacy. I submit these comments in support of including BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon on the 503A Bulks List.

I. THE 2023 RESTRICTION CAUSED REAL HARM

When the FDA moved these peptides to Category 2 in 2023, patients did not stop seeking them. They shifted demand away from licensed, quality-controlled 503A pharmacies toward unregulated online sources, overseas gray-market suppliers, and research-chemical vendors with no accountability, no sterility testing, and no physician oversight. The restriction did not reduce patient exposure -- it redirected it to sources that cannot be regulated or monitored. That is the opposite of patient safety.

II. THE CLINICAL CASE IS SOUND

BPC-157: A synthetic pentadecapeptide with well-characterized mechanisms involving angiogenesis, nitric oxide modulation, and GI cytoprotection. Its nominated indication -- ulcerative colitis -- represents a serious unmet need for patients who have failed or cannot tolerate conventional biologics. The science supports evaluation, not categorical prohibition.

TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment): An endogenous peptide involved in actin sequestration, cellular migration, and tissue repair. Clinical use in wound healing and orthopedic injury recovery has been consistent with preclinical data. Properly compounded TB-500 under physician supervision has demonstrated positive outcomes with no documented serious adverse events in my patient population.

MOTS-c: A mitochondria-derived peptide with demonstrated activity in insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle glucose uptake. Its nominated indications -- obesity and osteoporosis -- represent major public health burdens. Licensed compounding access would channel clinical use toward supervised, accountable settings.

Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon: Each has a legitimate scientific literature base supporting mechanism and clinical application. Prohibition of compliant compounding does not eliminate patient access -- it eliminates safe, supervised access.

III. QUALITY CONCERNS ARGUE FOR REGULATED COMPOUNDING, NOT PROHIBITION

The FDA cited immunogenicity risks, impurities, and insufficient human data. These concerns are valid -- and they are precisely why compounding through licensed 503A pharmacies operating under USP <797> sterile standards, with FDA-registered bulk suppliers, third-party finished-product testing, and patient-specific prescriptions, is the appropriate regulatory response. A licensed pharmacy provides accountability that no gray-market source can match.

IV. RECOMMENDED CONDITIONS FOR INCLUSION

1. Bulk substances sourced from FDA-registered suppliers with full Certificate of Analysis.
2. Independent third-party testing of finished preparations, documented in batch records.
3. Dispensing only pursuant to a valid patient-specific prescription with documented clinical rationale.
4. Adverse event tracking maintained by compounding pharmacies to build the evidence base.

These conditions are consistent with existing 503A standards. They represent responsible access, not unrestricted access.

V. CONCLUSION

Patients are already accessing these peptides. The only question is whether they access them through a licensed, regulated, accountable pharmacy -- or through an uncontrolled source. Inclusion on the 503A Bulks List answers that question in favor of patient safety.

I respectfully urge the Committee to recommend inclusion of all seven peptides under review on the 503A Bulks List.

Submitted by: A licensed 503A compounding pharmacy director

To the Food and Drug Administration,

I am writing on behalf of TruPeptide (trupeptide.co), an independent, non-commercial platform providing compound profiles, FDA status tracking, and consumer education on peptide therapies. We do not sell peptides or accept vendor payments.

1. Restricted access drove consumers to unregulated sources

When peptides were placed on the Category 2 list in 2023, demand did not decrease — it migrated to gray-market research chemical vendors with no physician oversight, inconsistent purity, and no adverse event reporting. Independent testing has repeatedly found gray-market peptide products to be mislabeled, underdosed, or contaminated. The Category 2 restriction did not reduce peptide use. It reduced the safety of peptide use.

HHS Secretary Kennedy stated on April 15, 2026: "This action begins to restore regulated access and will immediately begin shifting demand away from the black market." The question before this committee is whether the evidence supports formalizing that shift through the 503A bulks list.

2. The April 2026 removal was procedural — PCAC is the right venue

The removal of 12 peptides from Category 2 was triggered by withdrawal of the underlying nominations, not a substantive FDA safety determination. The July PCAC review is the appropriate venue for that evaluation, and we welcome it. Prolonged ambiguity benefits no one except unregulated suppliers.

3. Regulated infrastructure is already being built

In February 2025, Hims & Hers acquired a California-based peptide manufacturing facility in anticipation of regulated access. A positive PCAC recommendation would not create new demand — it would redirect existing demand from unregulated sources to regulated ones.

4. Requests to the committee

Publish evaluation criteria clearly so pharmacies and clinicians understand the basis for decisions.
Consider real-world alternatives: the relevant comparison is compounded peptide from a regulated pharmacy versus an unregulated overseas product with no quality controls.
For compounds where evidence is insufficient, specify what evidence would be needed — creating a pathway rather than a permanent exclusion.
Weigh international clinical experience for compounds like BPC-157 and TB-500, not only US-based trials.
Conclusion

The committee's decisions in 2026–2027 will determine whether consumers access these compounds through regulated channels with physician oversight, or through a gray market the FDA cannot effectively monitor. We urge regulatory clarity and a pragmatic assessment of real-world alternatives.

Respectfully submitted,

Nick Freidline
Founder, TruPeptide LLC
trupeptide.co
editorial@trupeptide.co

I am submitting this comment to share my personal experience with BPC-157, KPV, and TB-500.

I am a healthy 31-year-old male, 6’4” and 230 lbs, 12% body fat, and I recently completed an 8-week peptide protocol that included BPC-157, KPV, and TB-500 as part of my recovery efforts for a shoulder issue. My purpose in submitting this comment is not to make claims beyond my own experience, but to let FDA know that these compounds had a meaningful positive impact for me and deserve careful, open-minded review rather than dismissal based only on the current lack of large human datasets.

In my case, the combination was well tolerated and subjectively helpful. Over the course of the cycle, I experienced improved shoulder comfort, better day-to-day function, and a smoother recovery process than I had before starting. I did not experience obvious adverse effects that I could attribute to these compounds during the period I used them. From my perspective, the overall result was positive enough that I believe access to appropriately compounded versions should remain available while FDA continues evaluating safety, quality, and appropriate use cases.

I understand that personal experience is not the same as a controlled clinical trial. At the same time, patient and user experience can still provide useful real-world information, especially for compounds being explored for wound healing and inflammatory conditions. My experience suggests that KPV and TB-500 may have real value in supporting tissue recovery, and that BPC-157 also warrants continued scientific and regulatory attention.

I encourage FDA to consider that there are individuals who have used these compounds responsibly and have had meaningful positive outcomes. Rather than removing access entirely, I would urge the agency to support a path that emphasizes product quality, purity, proper compounding standards, and continued evidence development.

Thank you for considering this comment.
